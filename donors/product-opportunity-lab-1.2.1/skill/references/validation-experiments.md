# Assumptions, value of information, and validation experiments

## Critical assumptions

Map beliefs into:
- problem/desirability;
- value/resource commitment; for commercial opportunities, budget/willingness to pay;
- switching/adoption;
- distribution/acquisition;
- feasibility/data/integration;
- trust/security/compliance;
- economics/resource viability;
- retention/recurrence.

## Prioritize information, not activity

Use an ordinal value-of-information heuristic:

`priority ≈ decision impact × uncertainty × discriminatory power ÷ cost/time/irreversibility`

Score components qualitatively or 1–5. Do not pretend this is a calibrated expected-value calculation unless probabilities and payoffs are genuinely modeled.

A high-priority test should be capable of changing the decision. Testing a convenient low-risk assumption while a fatal unknown remains unresolved is failure.

## Test card

Before running a test define:
- **Hypothesis** — precise, discrete, falsifiable.
- **Method** — observable behavior/data to collect.
- **Metric** — what is counted.
- **Pass bar** — defined before results.
- **Fail/pivot bar** — what changes the thesis.
- **Sample/segment** — who/what is tested.
- **Confounds** — what could mislead.
- **Cost/time/irreversibility**.
- **Next action** — pass/fail/inconclusive.

## Evidence strength is claim-specific

Prefer behavior that resembles the target claim:
- problem interview/observation can strongly validate the workflow but weakly validate price;
- a job posting validates budget for labor but not willingness to buy software;
- a paid pilot strongly validates solution commitment but not long-term retention;
- renewal/repeated paid use is strong retention evidence.

## Typical progression

Early uncertainty → fast/cheap discovery evidence.
Later investment decisions → stronger behavioral/commitment evidence.

Possible tests include past-behavior interviews, workflow observation, truthful fake-door/CTA, concierge/manual delivery, prototype use, procurement steps, deposits/preorders, paid pilots, transactions, and renewal tests. Match the test to the business model and critical claim.

## Experimental build vs production build

A prototype may be justified at R2/R3 when it is the cheapest way to learn. Keep it deliberately narrow and reversible. Do not let "we need a prototype to test" turn into weeks of production architecture.

## Ethical constraints

No fake testimonials, deceptive scarcity, unauthorized data collection, misleading product availability, hidden non-refundable charges, or manipulation that would invalidate consent. Experiments must respect privacy, platform rules, and applicable law.

## Adaptive exit from experimentation

After every experiment, choose one:
- **DECIDE** — evidence crosses the predeclared bar strongly enough for the current decision;
- **ESCALATE EVIDENCE** — next investment is larger/less reversible, so use a stronger target-behavior test;
- **CHANGE METHOD** — the test was weak/confounded or two similar tests were inconclusive;
- **RECYCLE** — problem survives but segment/offer/channel/solution changes;
- **HOLD/KILL** — evidence is insufficient or negative enough that another experiment is not currently worth its cost.

Do not run a planned sequence of experiments after an earlier test has already made the next decision stable.
